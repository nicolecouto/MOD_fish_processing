# From t1_volt to chi: a worked walkthrough

[From raw volts to a trustworthy wavenumber spectrum](spectral_filtering_and_noise_floors.md) is this repo's concept-level *reference* for the calibrate → deconvolve → relabel chain - formulas, a reference table, one worked-example figure. This page is its narrative companion: the same chain, walked through more slowly, with the "why" spelled out for each piece rather than just the formula. If you already know this chain, the other page is faster to scan; if you're building intuition for the first time, start here.

**Scope**: this page stops once a trustworthy wavenumber spectrum exists and chi has been computed from it, either by direct integration (`chi_obs`) or by fitting the Batchelor curve's amplitude (`chi_mle`). A natural follow-up - taking that fitted/assumed curve, running it *backward* through the filters, and comparing the reconstructed raw spectrum against real bench-noise measurements - is planned as a separate page once that comparison has been done against real data.

## 1. What do the filters actually look like?

Two filters get divided out of a raw FP07 voltage spectrum before it's trustworthy: the AFE's own electronics/ADC response, and the thermistor bead's own thermal lag. Before looking at real data, it's worth seeing what these two filters actually do to a frequency axis.

![Three panels: (1) a generic sinc^n family showing how a higher power steepens the rolloff while keeping the same first crossing; (2) this repo's actual electronics/ADC filter, amplitude (sinc^4) vs. the squared power form that actually gets divided out; (3) the FP07 thermal-lag filter across two time constants and two fall speeds, showing both a longer tau and a slower fall speed steepen the rolloff](images/fpo7_filters_explained.png)

Generated by `plots/MODplot_fpo7_filters_explained.m` - purely illustrative, no real data, reusing `mod_scan_adc_filter.m`/`mod_scan_fpo7_transfer_function.m` directly rather than reimplementing their math.

**Panel 1 - sinc^n family.** A plain `sinc(f)` is 1 at `f=0` and crosses zero for the first time at `f=1` (in whatever units `f` is normalized to). Raising it to a higher power keeps that same first crossing but makes everything *around* it steeper - and, usefully, `sinc(f)^2`/`sinc(f)^4` stay non-negative instead of ringing below zero the way plain `sinc(f)` does. That's the shape being borrowed, at n=4, for the electronics filter.

**Panel 2 - the real electronics/ADC filter.** `mod_scan_adc_filter.m` builds `H_adc = sinc(f / (2*f(end)))^4` - the `2*f(end)` denominator is what stretches panel 1's "first crossing at 1" out past this spectrum's own Nyquist frequency (`f(end)`), so the filter only meaningfully attenuates within the band the ADC actually samples rather than crossing zero inside it. `mod_scan_fpo7_volts_to_Tg_spectrum.m` divides by the *squared* version (`electronics_filter = H_adc^2`, a power/PSD quantity, not an amplitude one) - visibly steeper than the bare `H_adc` curve above it, same "squaring steepens" effect as panel 1.

**Panel 3 - the FP07 thermal-lag filter.** `mod_scan_fpo7_transfer_function.m` computes `tau = tau0 * |w|^exponent` (`exponent = -0.32`, so `tau` *shrinks* as fall speed `w` grows), then `H = 1 / (1 + (2*pi*tau*f)^2)`. Two things steepen this filter, and neither one is obvious on first look:

- **A longer `tau0`** (red vs. blue above) - a physically slower-responding bead takes longer to equilibrate, so it rolls off sooner. Unsurprising.
- **A *slower* fall speed** (dashed vs. solid above) - this one runs against a common first guess. Going slower through the water doesn't mean "more time to resolve detail" the way a slower camera shutter might; the thermistor bead's own heat exchange with the surrounding water depends on flow speed past it, and *faster* flow drives faster convective heat exchange, so a *faster*-falling probe actually tracks the water better (smaller effective `tau`, less rolloff). Once the spectrum is relabeled onto a wavenumber axis (`k = f/w`), a slower fall speed does end up resolving more wavenumbers for a given frequency range - but that's a separate effect of the `k=f/w` relabeling itself, not of the filter shown here getting any gentler.

2. **Panel 2** is panel 2's own filter from Section 1 above, drawn again here at this scan's actual sample rate - the thing panel 1's spectrum is about to be divided by.
3. **Panel 3** shows the effect of the *first* division: calibrate to °C² (multiply by `volts_to_C(1)^2`) and divide out the electronics/ADC filter only - the thermal-lag rolloff is still in there.
4. **Panel 4** is the thermal-lag filter itself (Section 1's panel 3, for this specific scan's `w`) - the second thing panel 3's spectrum is about to be divided by.
5. **Panel 5** is the result of dividing panel 3 by panel 4 - fully deconvolved, in physical units, still on a frequency axis. This is checked to match `mod_scan_fpo7_volts_to_Tg_spectrum.m`'s own combined-division `Pt_T_f` to floating-point precision - splitting one division into two visible steps here doesn't change the arithmetic.
6. **Panel 6** is panel 5 relabeled onto a wavenumber axis (Section 3 below has the full derivation of why that's a `(2*pi*k)^2 * w` multiplication, not just a units change), with each scan's own best-fit Batchelor curve overlaid.

**What panel 6 doesn't show**: a noise-floor cutoff. Every real FP07 spectrum eventually drops into the sensor's own electronic noise, and trusting the deconvolved spectrum past that point is actively wrong - dividing noise by a rolling-off filter *amplifies* it, so the noise floor rises fastest exactly where the signal is weakest. Selecting that cutoff (`mod_scan_fpo7_cutoff.m`) works by comparing the raw, smoothed spectrum against a bench-measured noise curve (`mod_scan_fpo7_bench_noise_f.m`) rescaled to this scan's own high-frequency level (`mod_scan_fpo7_noise_adjust.m`), then taking the last frequency bin that still sits at least `SN_min=3` times above it - see [Section 4 of the reference page](spectral_filtering_and_noise_floors.md#4-where-the-noise-floor-comes-in) for the full mechanics and the contamination-frequency cap. `plots/MODplot_chi_spectra_noise_floor.m` is the dedicated diagnostic for this specific step (spectrum vs. noise floor vs. cutoff, three noise-floor variants side by side) - see its [visualization catalog entry](../visualization/index.md#modplot_chi_spectra_noise_floorm) - no example image is committed for it yet.

## 3. Why `(2*pi*k)^2`? Taylor's hypothesis and the frequency-to-wavenumber derivative

Panel 6 above didn't just relabel panel 5's x-axis from Hz to cpm - it also multiplied by `(2*pi*k)^2 * w`. Both factors are doing real, separate work.

**Time → space.** The probe never measures `T(z)` directly - it measures `T(t)` as it falls. Taylor's frozen-turbulence hypothesis says that if the turbulence itself evolves slowly compared to how fast the probe falls through it, the water can be treated as a frozen spatial pattern the probe sweeps past at its own fall speed `w`: a feature taking time `dt` to pass occupies a distance `dz = w*dt`, i.e. `z = w*t`. That substitution turns a frequency axis into a wavenumber axis, `k = f/w`, and (preserving total variance, `Phi(f)*df = Phi(k)*dk` with `df = w*dk`) turns a frequency-domain spectrum into a wavenumber-domain one via a single multiplication:

```
Phi_T(k) = Phi_T(f) * w
```

This step alone only changes *which axis* the spectrum is measured against - it doesn't yet turn temperature into temperature *gradient*. That's the second factor.

**Temperature → temperature gradient.** Write `T(z)` as a sum of complex exponentials (a Fourier synthesis), one per wavenumber: `T(z) = integral[ C_T(k) * exp(i*2*pi*k*z) ] dk`, where `C_T(k)` is a complex number - its magnitude is that wavenumber's amplitude, its phase is where along `z` that component actually peaks. Differentiating both sides with respect to `z` only touches the exponential (`d/dz[exp(i*2*pi*k*z)] = i*2*pi*k*exp(i*2*pi*k*z)`), so:

```
dT/dz = integral[ (i*2*pi*k) * C_T(k) * exp(i*2*pi*k*z) ] dk
```

i.e. differentiating multiplies each wavenumber's complex amplitude by `i*2*pi*k`. The `i` is a 90-degree phase rotation - it doesn't change how much power is at a given `k`. The magnitude `2*pi*k` does: steeper wiggles at higher wavenumber contribute more to the derivative, which is why a gradient spectrum is always shifted toward high `k` relative to the field it came from, and why squaring that factor - `(2*pi*k)^2` - is what turns a temperature *variance* spectrum into a temperature-*gradient*-variance spectrum. A single sinusoid makes the amplitude scaling concrete without the integral: `d/dz[cos(2*pi*k*z)] = -2*pi*k*sin(2*pi*k*z)`, i.e. the same wiggle, scaled by `2*pi*k`, phase-shifted a quarter cycle.

**Where the squaring actually happens.** A power spectral density is `Phi_T(k) = (1/L) * <|C_T(k)|^2>` - magnitude-squared (phase discarded, since chi only ever needs variance, not where in space a wiggle sits), averaged over many independent estimates, normalized by record length. That averaging-and-squaring is exactly what `pwelch` already did, back when `Pt_volt_f`/`Pt_T_f` were first built (split into overlapping segments, FFT each one, square each one's magnitude, average across segments). That's the *only* place real data gets squared anywhere in this chain. Everything from there on - `* w` for Taylor's hypothesis, `.^2` on `(2*pi*k)` for the derivative - is multiplication by a real, deterministic conversion factor applied to an already-built PSD, not a second pass of squaring real data.

Putting both factors together, with units checked out explicitly:

```
(2*pi*k)^2 * Phi_T(f) * w   ~   (1/m)^2 * (degC^2 / (1/s)) * (m/s)   =   degC^2 / m
```

which is exactly `mod_scan_fpo7_volts_to_Tg_spectrum.m` step 3: `k = f/abs(w); Pt_Tg_k = (2*pi*k).^2 .* Pt_T_f * abs(w)`.

## 4. Two ways to turn that spectrum into chi

Once a trustworthy `Pt_Tg_k(k)` and a cutoff `kc` exist, there are two different ways this repo turns them into a single number:

**`chi_obs` - direct integration.** Osborn & Cox (1972)'s original definition, applied directly to the windowed data: sum the observed spectrum between a fixed low-wavenumber bound (`kmin_obs`, default 3 cpm) and `kc`, scaled by `6*ktemp`:

```
chi_obs = 6 * ktemp * dk * sum(Pt_Tg_k(kmin_obs <= k <= kc))
```

This only ever sees what's actually in `[kmin_obs, kc]` - it has no opinion about what's happening below `kmin_obs` (deliberately excluded, since real spectra depart from the Batchelor shape at low wavenumber - leftover large-scale structure, not turbulence) or past `kc` (noise-dominated, not necessarily zero physically, just untrustworthy).

**`chi_mle` - fit the Batchelor curve's amplitude instead.** Rather than summing the raw (noisy) data, fit the *amplitude* of a theoretical Batchelor curve (its shape fixed by this scan's `epsilon`/`nu`/`ktemp`) to the same `[kmin_obs, kc]` window, via a grid-search Maximum Likelihood fit (Ruddick, Anis & Thompson 2000). Because the Batchelor curve is normalized so that `chi` is defined as its integral over *all* wavenumbers (not just the observed window), whatever amplitude best explains the windowed data comes with an implied total-variance value that includes the part past `kc` direct integration was forced to throw away - typically ~1.8-2x `chi_obs` on real ASTRAL data.

Both estimators, the grid-search mechanics, the registry of tunable parameters (`kmin_obs`, `sn_min`, `time_constant_s`, ...), and real validation numbers against ASTRAL/BLT data are documented in full in [FP07 calibration and chi: the time-constant deconvolution](../workflow/L2_calc_chi.md) - this page stops at explaining *what* the two estimators are doing and why they differ; that page is the place for exact function signatures, `metadata` field provenance, and known limitations (no chi figure-of-merit yet, among others).
